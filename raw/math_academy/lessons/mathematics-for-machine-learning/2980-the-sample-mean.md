## Lesson

### Introduction

In statistics, we often want to use sample data to deduce (or **infer**) the values of various **population parameters**. The two most common population parameters that we wish to find are

- the **population mean** $\mu,$ and

- the **population variance** $\sigma^2.$

The population mean is the mean of the *entire* population. Likewise, the population variance is the variance of the *entire* population. We should consider these values as *fixed* unknowns.

Suppose, for example, we want to find the mean number of pages per book at a particular library. In other words, we wish to find the population mean $\mu.$ However, it is impractical to count the pages in every book. So, instead, we might approximate $\mu$ using the following sampling procedure:

1. Randomly choose, say, $5$ books off the shelf.

2. Count the number of pages in each book.

3. Compute the mean of the $5$ observations.

In this case, the **sample size** $n=5.$

Suppose we follow this procedure and get the following **observations**:

$$


201, \qquad 506, \qquad 58, \qquad 104, \qquad 99


$$

It's convenient to denote the individual observations as $x_i.$ So, in this case, we have

$$


x_1 = 201,\qquad x_2 = 506,\qquad \ldots,\qquad x_5 = 99.


$$

We can compute the mean $\overline{x}$ of our sample as follows:

$$


\begin{aligned}\overset{𝑥}{} & =\frac{1}{𝑛}\underset{\underset{𝑖=1}{∑}}{\overset{}{𝑛}}𝑥_{𝑖} \\ & =\frac{1}{5}(201+506+58+104+99) \\ & =\frac{1}{5}⋅968 \\ & =193.6\end{aligned}


$$

The value $\bar{x} = 193.6$ is called an **estimate** of the population mean $\mu.$ We sometimes write $\hat{\mu} = 193.6$ to denote that the value $193.6$ is an estimate of $\mu.$

It's important to realize that our estimate $\bar{x}$ depends entirely on this particular sample. We'd get a different estimate for $\mu$ if we took a different sample.

### The Sample Mean

It is helpful to think of the observations of a particular sample

$$


x_1,\quad x_2,\quad \ldots,\quad x_n


$$

as a particular instance of data generated by a sequence of random variables

$$


X_1,\quad X_2,\quad \ldots,\quad X_n.


$$

Let's make sure we're clear about the notation:

- $X_i$ is a *random variable* that denotes the $i$th observation in a random sample. Its value changes when we conduct a new sample.

- $x_i$ is the value of the $i$th observation in a particular sample.

We typically assume that our sequence of random variables $X_1, X_2,\ldots, X_n$ has the following properties:

- They are **independent and identically distributed** (or **I.I.D.**), meaning they are independent and have the same probability distribution. Moreover, they have the same distribution as the population.

- Their mean is equal to the population mean:

- Their variance is equal to the population variance:

$$


\textrm{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2, \qquad 1\leq i\leq n


$$

We often refer to a sequence of random variables $X_1, X_2,\ldots, X_n$ with these properties as a **random sample of size** $\boldsymbol{n}.$

In general, if $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_{n}$ is a random sample of size $n,$ then the **sample mean** is given by

$$


\overline{X} = \dfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i .


$$

We say that the sample mean $\overline{X}$ is an **estimator** of the population mean $\mu.$

Since $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ are random variables, so is $\overline{X}.$ The probability distribution of $\overline{X}$ is called the **sampling distribution** of $\overline{X}.$ The sampling distribution of $\overline{X}$ is an important topic we'll discuss in detail in a future lesson.

### Example: Estimating a Population Mean From the Sample Mean

#### Question

Suppose the following data is sampled from a population. Compute an estimate for the population mean.

$$


6, \quad 13, \quad 7, \quad 5, \quad 14 , \quad 11, \quad 7


$$

#### Explanation

Suppose $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ is a sequence of random variables representing a random sample of size $n$ drawn from a population with the population mean $\mu.$ Then, the sample mean $\overline{X}$ is given by

$$


\overline{X} = \dfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i.


$$

Note that:

- $\overline{X}$ is the mean value of $X_1,X_2,\ldots, X_n,$ while $\bar{x}$ is the mean of a specific sample $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n,$

- the sample mean $\overline{X}$ is an estimator of the population mean $\mu,$ and

- the value $\hat{\mu} = \bar{x}$ is an estimate of the population mean $\mu.$

Therefore, our estimate for the population mean is

$$


\begin{aligned}\overset{𝜇}{^} & =\overset{𝑥}{} \\ & =\frac{1}{𝑛}\underset{\underset{𝑖=1}{∑}}{\overset{}{7}}𝑥_{𝑖} \\ & =\frac{1}{7}(6+13+7+5+14+11+7) \\ & =\frac{63}{7} \\ & =9.\end{aligned}


$$

### The Sample Mean as an Unbiased Estimator

One important feature of $\overline{X}$ is that it's an **unbiased estimator** of $\mu.$ This means that the expected value of $\overline{X}$ equals $\mu,$ that is

$$


\textrm{E}[\overline{X}] = \mu.


$$

We can easily show that $\overline{X}$ is an unbiased estimator of $\mu$ using the properties of expectation:

$$


\begin{aligned}E[\overset{𝑋}{}] & =E[\frac{1}{𝑛}\underset{\underset{𝑖=1}{∑}}{\overset{}{𝑛}}𝑋_{𝑖}] \\ & =\frac{1}{𝑛}⋅E[\underset{\underset{𝑖=1}{∑}}{\overset{}{𝑛}}𝑋_{𝑖}] \\ & =\frac{1}{𝑛}⋅E[𝑋_{1}+𝑋_{2}+⋯+𝑋_{𝑛}] \\ & =\frac{1}{𝑛}⋅(E[𝑋_{1}]+E[𝑋_{2}]+⋯+E[𝑋_{𝑛}]) \\ & =\frac{1}{𝑛}⋅(𝜇+𝜇+⋯+𝜇) \\ & =\frac{1}{𝑛}⋅𝑛𝜇 \\ & =𝜇\end{aligned}


$$

The fact that $\overline{X}$ is an unbiased estimator of $\mu$ is a desirable property. We'll meet so-called **biased estimators** in future lessons.

Finally, any estimate $\bar{x}$ of the population mean $\mu$ found by computing the mean of a sample is called an **unbiased estimate** of $\mu.$

### Example: The Expected Value of the Sample Mean

#### Question

A sample $X_1, X_2$ of two metallic rings is drawn from a population. The population has rings of two different diameters, $16.5\,\textrm{mm}$ and $\textrm{18}\,\textrm{mm},$ distributed in the ratio $5:1.$ Calculate $\textrm{E}[\overline{X}].$

#### Explanation

Suppose $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ is a sequence of random variables representing a random sample of size $n$ drawn from a population with population mean $\mu.$ Then, the sample mean $\overline{X}$ is given by

$$


\overline{X} = \dfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i.


$$

Since $\overline{X}$ is an unbiased estimator of $\mu,$ we have

$$


\textrm{E}[\overline{X}] = \mu.


$$

We're told that the rings in the population have diameters $16.5\,\textrm{mm}$ and $\textrm{18}\,\textrm{mm},$ and these are distributed in the ratio $5:1.$ Therefore, to compute the population mean, we can consider a population consisting of $5+1 = 6$ rings only with the following diameters:

$$


16.5\,\textrm{mm}, \qquad 16.5\,\textrm{mm}, \qquad 16.5\,\textrm{mm}, \qquad 16.5\,\textrm{mm}, \qquad 16.5\,\textrm{mm}, \qquad 18\,\textrm{mm}.


$$

Therefore, the population mean is

$$


\begin{aligned}𝜇 & =\frac{1}{6}\underset{𝑖}{∑}𝑥_{𝑖} \\ & =\frac{1}{6}(5⋅16.5+18) \\ & =\frac{1}{6}(82.5+18) \\ & =\frac{100.5}{6} \\ & =16.75\,mm.\end{aligned}


$$

Finally,

$$


\textrm{E}[\overline{X}] = \mu = 16.75\,\textrm{mm}.


$$
