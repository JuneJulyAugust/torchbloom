### Introduction

When solving an equation, we sometimes require taking the square root of a negative number. In such cases, we will not end up with real solutions, but imaginary solutions.

To demonstrate, let's solve the equation $2x^2 +8 = 0$ for $x.$ First, we rearrange the equation, as follows:

$$



\begin{aligned} 2x^2 + 8 &= 0 \\[3pt] 2x^2 &= -8 \\[3pt] x^2 &= -4 \end{aligned}



$$

By taking the square root, we obtain

$$



x = \pm \sqrt{-4}.



$$

We now rewrite $x$ using the imaginary unit $\textrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}\, \mathbin{:}$

$$



\begin{aligned}𝑥 & =±\sqrt{√−4} \\ & =±\sqrt{√4⋅(−1)} \\ & =±\sqrt{√4}⋅\sqrt{√−1} \\ & =±2i\end{aligned}



$$

Therefore, the solutions are $x = \pm 2 \textrm{i}.$

### Example: Solving a Quadratic Equation With Purely Imaginary Solutions

#### Question

What are the solutions to $x^2 + 16 = 0?$

#### Explanation

First, we rearrange the equation, as follows:

$$



\begin{aligned} x^2 + 16 &= 0 \\[3pt] x^2 &= -16 \end{aligned}



$$

By taking the square root, we obtain

$$



x = \pm \sqrt{-16}.



$$

We now rewrite $x$ using the imaginary unit $\textrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}\, \mathbin{:}$

$$



\begin{aligned}𝑥 & =±\sqrt{√−16} \\ & =±\sqrt{√16⋅(−1)} \\ & =±\sqrt{√16}⋅\sqrt{√−1} \\ & =±4i\end{aligned}



$$

Therefore, the solutions are $x = \pm 4 \textrm{i}.$

### Example: Solving a Quadratic Equation With Purely Imaginary Fractional Solutions

#### Question

If $16z^2 + 49 = 0,$ then $z =$

#### Explanation

First, we rearrange the equation, as follows:

$$



\begin{aligned} 16z^2 + 49 & = 0\\\[5pt] 16z^2 & = -49 \\\[5pt] z^2 & = -\dfrac{49}{16} \end{aligned}



$$

By taking the square root, we obtain

$$



z = \pm \sqrt{-\dfrac{49}{16}}.



$$

We now rewrite $z$ using the imaginary unit $\textrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}\, \mathbin{:}$

$$



\begin{aligned}𝑧 & =±\sqrt{√−\frac{49}{16}} \\ & =±\sqrt{√\frac{49}{16}⋅(−1)} \\ & =±\sqrt{√\frac{49}{16}}⋅\sqrt{√−1} \\ & =±\frac{\sqrt{√49}}{\sqrt{√16}}⋅\sqrt{√−1} \\ & =±\frac{7}{4}i\end{aligned}



$$

Therefore, the solutions are $z=\pm \dfrac{7}{4} \textrm{i}.$

### Example: Solving a Quadratic Equation With Purely Imaginary Fractional Roots With Solutions

#### Question

What are the solutions of the equation $2y^2 + 9 = 0?$

#### Explanation

First, we rearrange the equation, as follows:

$$



\begin{aligned} 2y^2 + 9 &= 0 \\\[5pt] 2y^2 &= -9\\\[5pt] y^2 & = -\dfrac{9}{2} \end{aligned}



$$

By taking square root, we obtain

$$



y = \pm \sqrt{-\dfrac{9}{2}}.



$$

We now rewrite $y$ using the imaginary unit $\textrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}\, \mathbin{:}$

$$



\begin{aligned}𝑦 & =±\sqrt{√−\frac{9}{2}} \\ & =±\sqrt{√\frac{9}{2}⋅(−1)} \\ & =±\sqrt{√\frac{9}{2}}⋅\sqrt{√−1} \\ & =±\frac{\sqrt{√9}}{\sqrt{√2}}⋅\sqrt{√−1} \\ & =±\frac{3}{\sqrt{√2}}i\end{aligned}



$$

Finally, we rationalize the denominator:

$$



\begin{aligned}𝑦 & =±\frac{3}{\sqrt{√2}}i \\ & =±\frac{3}{\sqrt{√2}}⋅\frac{\sqrt{√2}}{\sqrt{√2}}⋅\sqrt{√−1} \\ & =±\frac{3\sqrt{√2}}{2}i\end{aligned}



$$

Therefore, the solutions are $y=\pm \dfrac{3\sqrt{2}}{2} \textrm{i}.$
