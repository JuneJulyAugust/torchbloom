## Lesson

### Introduction

When dealing with sums or differences of square roots, we can combine any terms with the same algebraic expression under the square root symbol $\sqrt{\phantom 0}.$ This is similar to collecting like terms in a linear expression.

For example, consider the following expression:

$$



5\sqrt{x} - 3\sqrt{x}



$$

We can simplify our expression by combining the two radicals, as follows:

$$



\begin{aligned}5\sqrt{√𝑥}−3\sqrt{√𝑥} & = \\ (5−3)\sqrt{√𝑥} & = \\ 2\sqrt{√𝑥} & \end{aligned}



$$

We *cannot* combine terms when the expressions under the radical are different. For example, the expression

$$



5\sqrt{x} - 3\sqrt{y}



$$

cannot be simplified because there is no way of combining $\sqrt x$ and $\sqrt y.$

### Example: Simplifying Expressions Containing Radicals

#### Question

Simplify $9\sqrt{p - 4} - 7\sqrt{p - 4}.$

#### Explanation

We can combine the two $\sqrt{p - 4}$ terms together, as follows:

$$



\begin{aligned}9\sqrt{√𝑝−4}−7\sqrt{√𝑝−4} & = \\ (9−7)\sqrt{√𝑝−4} & = \\ 2\sqrt{√𝑝−4} & \end{aligned}



$$

### The Product Rule for Radicals

Recall that the product rule for radicals states that

$$



\sqrt{a\cdot b} = \sqrt{a}\cdot \sqrt{b}



$$

where $a$ and $b$ are nonnegative.

We can use the product rule for radicals to simplify some expressions. Let's see an example.

### Example: Simplifying Sums of Radicals Using the Product Rule

#### Question

Simplify $\sqrt{12x}-\sqrt{27x}.$

#### Explanation

We simplify each of the radical terms using the product rule:

$$



\begin{aligned}\sqrt{√12𝑥}−\sqrt{√27𝑥} & = \\ \sqrt{√4⋅3𝑥}−\sqrt{√9⋅3𝑥} & = \\ \sqrt{√4}⋅\sqrt{√3𝑥}−\sqrt{√9}⋅\sqrt{√3𝑥} & = \\ 2⋅\sqrt{√3𝑥}−3⋅\sqrt{√3𝑥} & = \\ 2\sqrt{√3𝑥}−3\sqrt{√3𝑥} & \end{aligned}



$$

Finally, we combine like terms:

$$



\begin{aligned}2\sqrt{√3𝑥}−3\sqrt{√3𝑥} & = \\ (2−3)⋅\sqrt{√3𝑥} & = \\ (−1)⋅\sqrt{√3𝑥} & = \\ −\sqrt{√3𝑥} & \end{aligned}



$$

### Simplifying Expressions with Cube Roots

We can simplify cube root expressions in the same way as with square roots.

For example, consider the following expression:

$$



4\sqrt[3]x - 2\sqrt[3] x



$$

We can simplify our expression by combining the two radicals, as follows:

$$



\begin{aligned}4\sqrt[√𝑥]{3}−2\sqrt[√𝑥]{3} & = \\ (4−2)\sqrt[√𝑥]{3} & = \\ 2\sqrt[√𝑥]{3} & \end{aligned}



$$

As with square roots, we *cannot* combine terms when the expressions under the radical are different. For example, the expression

$$



\sqrt[3]{x-1} + \sqrt[3]{x+1}



$$

cannot be simplified because there is no way of combining $\sqrt[3]{x-1}$ and $\sqrt[3]{x+1}.$

Finally, it is not possible to combine square root and cube root terms, even if the numbers under the radical symbol are the same! For example, the expression

$$



2\sqrt x + \sqrt[3] x



$$

cannot be simplified because there is no way of combining $\sqrt x$ and $\sqrt[3] x.$

### Example: Simplifying Sums of Radicals with Cube Roots

#### Question

Simplify $10\sqrt[3]{y} + 2\sqrt[3]{y} - 7\sqrt{y}.$

#### Explanation

We can combine the two $\sqrt[3]{y}$ terms together, but we cannot combine the $\sqrt[3]{y}$ and $\sqrt{y}$ terms:

$$



\begin{aligned} 10\sqrt[3]{y} + 2\sqrt[3]{y} - 7\sqrt{y} &= \\\[5pt] (10 + 2)\sqrt[3]{y} - 7\sqrt{y} &= \\\[5pt] 12\sqrt[3]{y}-7\sqrt{y} & \end{aligned}



$$
