## Lesson

### Introduction

In this lesson, we will learn how to construct linear inequalities to model real-world situations.

Suppose Anna earns $20$ per hour and needs *at least* $ 420$ each week to cover her living expenses. We wish to construct an inequality to model this situation.

First, we let $h$ be the total number of hours Anna works per week.

- Since Anna earns $20$ per hour, she will earn $20h$ dollars in total each week.

- Since she must earn *at least* $ 420$ each week, the amount of money she earns must be *greater than or equal to* $420.$

So, the number of hours $h$ that Anna needs to work each week must satisfy the following inequality:

$$


20h \geq 420


$$

### Translating Phrases Into "Or Equal To" Inequalities

An essential part of modeling with inequalities is translating sentences into math using the correct inequality symbol.

The following phrases typically use "greater than or equal to" ($\geq$) inequalities:

- at least

- no less than

- no smaller than

In the last example, we saw that Anna must earn *at least* $420,$ which gave the "greater than or equal to" inequality

$$


20h \geq 420.


$$

On the other hand, the following phrases typically use "less than or equal to" ($\leq$) inequalities:

- at most

- no more than

- no greater than

For example, if a door frame is $y$ meters tall, yet it can be *no greater than* $2$ meters high, then

$$


y\leq 2.


$$

### Translating Phrases Into Strict Inequalities

We must also learn to construct strict inequalities to model situations.

The following phrases typically use the "greater than" $(>)$ symbol:

- greater than

- more than

- exceeds

- older than

For example, if a house's age is given by the variable $a,$ and it's known that the house is older than $100$ years old, then we have

$$


a > 100.


$$

On the other hand, the following phrases typically use the "less than" $(<)$ symbol:

- less than

- smaller than

- younger than

For example, if the height $h$ of a tree is less than $3$ meters, we have

$$


h \lt 3.


$$

Let's see some more examples.

### Example: Constructing an Inequality Using Multiplication

#### Question

Karen is preparing lemonade. Each glass of lemonade requires $2$ tablespoons of sugar to make. If Karen wants to use less than $25$ tablespoons of sugar, what inequality must be satisfied by $G,$ the number of glasses of lemonade that Karen can make?

#### Explanation

Each glass of lemonade requires $2$ tablespoons of sugar. So, for $G$ glasses of lemonade, Karen will need to use $2G$ tablespoons of sugar.

Since Karen wants to spend ** $25$ tablespoons on sugar, the following inequality must be satisfied:

$$


2G < 25


$$

### Example: Constructing an Inequality Using Addition

#### Question

Peter spends $h$ hours studying for his upcoming math test. Betty spends no more than $9$ hours studying, and she spends one hour more than Peter. Find an inequality that must be satisfied by $h.$

#### Explanation

Peter spends $h$ hours studying, and Betty studies for one hour more than Peter. Therefore, Betty spends $h+1$ hours studying.

Since Betty spends ** $9$ hours studying, the following inequality must be satisfied:

$$


h+1 \leq 9


$$

### Example: Constructing an Inequality Using Multiplication and Addition

#### Question

Tariq is purchasing software licenses for his company. Each license costs $62,$ and there is a one-time setup fee of $85.$ If his total spending exceeds $700,$ what inequality describes the number of licenses ($\ell$) Tariq can purchase?

#### Explanation

Each license costs $62.$ So, if Tariq purchases $\ell$ licenses, he pays $62\ell$ dollars for the licenses.

There is also a one-time setup fee of $85.$ Therefore, the total cost, in dollars, is

$$


62\ell + 85.


$$

Since Tariq’s total cost exceeds $700,$ the following inequality must be satisfied:

$$


62\ell + 85 > 700


$$
