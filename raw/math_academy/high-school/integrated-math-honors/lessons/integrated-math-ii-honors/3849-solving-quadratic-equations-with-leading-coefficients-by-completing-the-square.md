### Introduction

We can use the method of completing the square to solve quadratic equations with leading coefficients.

As an example, let's solve the following quadratic equation by completing the square:

$$


4x^2-8x+3 = 0


$$

First, we move the constant term to the right-hand side and complete the square on the left-hand side.

$$


\begin{aligned}4𝑥^{2}−8𝑥+3 & =0 \\ 4𝑥^{2}−8𝑥 & =−3 \\ 4(𝑥^{2}−2𝑥) & =−3 \\ 4(𝑥^{2}−2⋅1⋅𝑥+1^{2}−1^{2}) & =−3 \\ 4[(𝑥−1)^{2}−1] & =−3\end{aligned}


$$

Now, expanding the brackets, we get

$$


\begin{aligned}4[(𝑥−1)^{2}−1] & =−3 \\ 4(𝑥−1)^{2}−4 & =−3.\end{aligned}


$$

Next, we isolate the perfect square by adding $4$ to both sides and then dividing by $4\mathbin{:}$

$$


\begin{aligned}4(𝑥−1)^{2}−4 & =−3 \\ 4(𝑥−1)^{2}−4+4 & =−3+4 \\ 4(𝑥−1)^{2} & =1 \\ (𝑥−1)^{2} & =\frac{1}{4}\end{aligned}


$$

Next, we solve the equation using the square root method:

$$


\begin{aligned}(𝑥−1)^{2} & =\frac{1}{4} \\ \sqrt{(𝑥−1)^{2}} & =\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}} \\ |𝑥−1| & =\frac{1}{2} \\ 𝑥−1 & =±\frac{1}{2} \\ 𝑥 & =1±\frac{1}{2}\end{aligned}


$$

Finally, we simplify the solutions:

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑥_{1} & =1−\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{2} \\ 𝑥_{2} & =1+\frac{1}{2}=\frac{3}{2}\end{aligned}


$$

Therefore, the solutions are $x=\dfrac12$ and $x=\dfrac32.$

### Example: Solving a Quadratic Equation Given a Corresponding Perfect Square

#### Question

Solve the equation $-2x^2 - 4x = 1.$

**

#### Explanation

Using the hint, we have

$$


x^2 + 2x + 1 = (x + 1)^2.


$$

First, we complete the square on the left-hand side:

$$


\begin{aligned}−2𝑥^{2}−4𝑥 & =1 \\ −2(𝑥^{2}+2𝑥) & =1 \\ −2(𝑥^{2}+2⋅1𝑥) & =1 \\ −2(𝑥^{2}+2⋅1𝑥+1^{2}−1^{2}) & =1 \\ −2[(𝑥+1)^{2}−1^{2}] & =1 \\ −2[(𝑥+1)^{2}−1] & =1 \\ −2(𝑥+1)^{2}+(−2)(−1) & =1 \\ −2(𝑥+1)^{2}+2 & =1\end{aligned}


$$

Isolating the perfect square by subtracting $2$ from both sides and then dividing by $(-2),$ we get

$$


\begin{aligned}−2(𝑥+1)^{2}+2 & =1 \\ −2(𝑥+1)^{2}+2−2 & =1−2 \\ −2(𝑥+1)^{2} & =−1 \\ (𝑥+1)^{2} & =\frac{(−1)}{(−2)} \\ (𝑥+1)^{2} & =\frac{1}{2}.\end{aligned}


$$

We now solve the equation using the square root method:

$$


\begin{aligned}(𝑥+1)^{2} & =\frac{1}{2} \\ \sqrt{(𝑥+1)^{2}} & =\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}} \\ |𝑥+1| & =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ 𝑥+1 & =±\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ 𝑥 & =−1±\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\end{aligned}


$$

### Example: Solving a Quadratic Equation With a Positive Leading Coefficient

#### Question

Write the equation $2x^2 - 6x = 1$ in the form $(x+p)^2 = q,$ and hence solve the equation.

#### Explanation

First, we divide out by the coefficient of the quadratic term, and complete the square on the left-hand side.

$$


\begin{aligned}2𝑥^{2}−6𝑥 & =1 \\ 2(𝑥^{2}−3𝑥) & =1 \\ 𝑥^{2}−3𝑥 & =\frac{1}{2} \\ 𝑥^{2}−2⋅\frac{3}{2}⋅𝑥 & =\frac{1}{2} \\ 𝑥^{2}−2⋅(\frac{3}{2})⋅𝑥+(\frac{3}{2})^{2}−(\frac{3}{2})^{2} & =\frac{1}{2} \\ (𝑥−\frac{3}{2})^{2}−(\frac{3}{2})^{2} & =\frac{1}{2}\end{aligned}


$$

Then, we isolate the perfect square:

$$


\begin{aligned}(𝑥−\frac{3}{2})^{2}−(\frac{3}{2})^{2} & =\frac{1}{2} \\ (𝑥−\frac{3}{2})^{2}−\frac{9}{4} & =\frac{2}{4} \\ (𝑥−\frac{3}{2})^{2} & =\frac{11}{4}\end{aligned}


$$

So, $p=-\dfrac32$ and $q=\dfrac{11}{4}.$ Now, we solve the equation using the square root method:

$$


\begin{aligned}\sqrt{(𝑥−\frac{3}{2})^{2}} & =\sqrt{\frac{11}{4}} \\ 𝑥−\frac{3}{2} & =\frac{\sqrt{11}}{2} \\ 𝑥−\frac{3}{2} & =±\frac{\sqrt{11}}{2} \\ 𝑥 & =\frac{3}{2}±\frac{\sqrt{11}}{2} \\ 𝑥 & =\frac{3±\sqrt{11}}{2}\end{aligned}


$$

### Example: Solving a Quadratic Equation With a Negative Leading Coefficient

#### Question

Write the equation $-2x^2+2x+1 = 0$ in the form $2(x+p)^2=q,$ and hence solve the equation.

#### Explanation

First, we multiply the equation by $-1$:

$$


\begin{aligned}(−1)⋅(−2𝑥^{2}+2𝑥+1) & =(−1)⋅0 \\ 2𝑥^{2}−2𝑥−1 & =0\end{aligned}


$$

Now, we move the constant term to the right-hand side, factor the coefficient of the quadratic term, and complete the square on the left-hand side.

$$


\begin{aligned}2𝑥^{2}−2𝑥−1 & =0 \\ 2𝑥^{2}−2𝑥 & =1 \\ 2(𝑥^{2}−𝑥) & =1 \\ 2[𝑥^{2}−2⋅\frac{1}{2}𝑥+(\frac{1}{2})^{2}−(\frac{1}{2})^{2}] & =1 \\ 2[(𝑥−\frac{1}{2})^{2}−\frac{1}{4}] & =1\end{aligned}


$$

Next, we expand the brackets, and write the equation in the required form:

$$


\begin{aligned}2[(𝑥−\frac{1}{2})^{2}−\frac{1}{4}] & =1 \\ 2(𝑥−\frac{1}{2})^{2}−\frac{1}{2} & =1 \\ 2(𝑥−\frac{1}{2})^{2} & =\frac{3}{2}\end{aligned}


$$

Therefore, $p=-\dfrac12$ and $q=\dfrac32.$ We now solve the equation using the square root method:

$$


\begin{aligned}2(𝑥−\frac{1}{2})^{2} & =\frac{3}{2} \\ (𝑥−\frac{1}{2})^{2} & =\frac{3}{4} \\ \sqrt{(𝑥−\frac{1}{2})^{2}} & =\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}} \\ 𝑥−\frac{1}{2} & =\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \\ 𝑥−\frac{1}{2} & =±\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \\ 𝑥 & =\frac{1}{2}±\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\end{aligned}


$$

### Deriving the Quadratic Formula

Consider the quadratic equation

$$


ax^2+bx+c = 0,


$$

where $a\neq 0.$ We can derive a general solution to this equation by completing the square.

First, let's subtract $c$ from both sides to isolate the variable terms:

$$


ax^2+bx = -c


$$

Since $a\neq 0,$ dividing by $a$ gives

$$


x^2 + \dfrac{b}{a}x = -\dfrac{c}{a}.


$$

Next, we complete the square on the left-hand side and move the constant terms to the right-hand side:

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑥^{2}+2⋅\frac{𝑏}{2𝑎}𝑥=−\frac{𝑐}{𝑎} & \\ (𝑥+\frac{𝑏}{2𝑎})^{2}−(\frac{𝑏}{2𝑎})^{2} & =−\frac{𝑐}{𝑎} \\ (𝑥+\frac{𝑏}{2𝑎})^{2}−\frac{𝑏^{2}}{4𝑎^{2}} & =−\frac{𝑐}{𝑎} \\ (𝑥+\frac{𝑏}{2𝑎})^{2} & =\frac{𝑏^{2}}{4𝑎^{2}}−\frac{𝑐}{𝑎}\end{aligned}


$$

Putting the terms on the right-hand side over a common factor of $4a^2,$ we get

$$


\left(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \dfrac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}.


$$

Taking the square root of both sides yields

$$


x + \dfrac{b}{2a} = \pm \sqrt{\dfrac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}}


$$

which can be simplified as follows:

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑥+\frac{𝑏}{2𝑎} & =±\frac{\sqrt{𝑏^{2}−4𝑎𝑐}}{2𝑎}\end{aligned}


$$

Finally, we isolate $x{:}$

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑥 & =−\frac{𝑏}{2𝑎}±\frac{\sqrt{𝑏^{2}−4𝑎𝑐}}{2𝑎}\end{aligned}


$$

which simplifies as

$$


x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.


$$

This formula is called the **quadratic formula**. You'll learn more about how to apply the quadratic formula in future lessons.
