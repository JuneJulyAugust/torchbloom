## Lesson

### Introduction

Suppose a rational function $f(x)$ has a vertical asymptote at $x=a.$ Let's imagine that we're approaching the asymptote *from the left*, so $x\to a^{-}.$

There are two possible behaviors that we can observe:

- $f(x)$ *increases* without bound as $x$ approaches $a$ from the left. In this case, we write $f(x)\to{\color{blue}{+\infty}}$ as $x\to a^{-}.$

- $f(x)$ *decreases* without bound as $x$ approaches $a$ from the left. In this case, we write $f(x)\to{\color{red}{-\infty}}$ as $x\to a^{-}.$

We can determine the unbounded behavior of rational functions near asymptotes by plotting them. However, plotting rational functions can be difficult.

To determine the behavior of a rational function near an asymptote *without* resorting to plots, we consider values of $f(x)$ close to the asymptote and deduce its behavior from there.

As an example, let's consider the following function:

$$


f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x-1}


$$

Note the following:

- $f(x)$ has a vertical asymptote at $x=1.$

- Therefore, $f(x)$ can approach *either* $\color{blue}\infty$ *or* $\color{red}-\infty$ as $x$ approaches $1$ from the left.

We can determine how $f(x)$ behaves as $x$ approaches $1$ from the left using a table of values like the one below.

As we move to the left in our table,

- the $x$-values get closer to $x=1,$ and

- the values of $f(x)$ are *negative*, and they *decrease* as we approach $x=1.$

Therefore, we conclude that

$$


f(x) \rightarrow {\color{red}{-\infty}} \quad \textrm{as}\quad x\rightarrow 1^-.


$$

**Note**: In our table, we calculated the value of $f(x)$ at four points. After some practice, you'll only need to compute $f(x)$ at one or two points to determine its behavior near an asymptote.
