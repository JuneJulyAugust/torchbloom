### Introduction

Sometimes, we want to find the probability that two or more events occur together. Events like this, involving two or more events, are called **compound events**.

For instance, suppose $100$ members of a volleyball club were asked about their preferences between Chinese food and Italian pizza. The results of the survey are represented in the two-way frequency table below.

Suppose that we want to find the probability that a randomly selected club member is a male who prefers pizza.

We can find this using the formula for the experimental probability:

$$



P(\text{Event}) = \dfrac{\text{number of event occurrences}}{\text{total number of trials}}



$$

To find the number of times both events occurred simultaneously, we identify where these two categories overlap in the table.

The cell where the row **"Male"** and the column **"Italian pizza"** meet is highlighted in the table below.

Dividing this number by the total number of data points in the table $(100),$ we get

$$



\begin{aligned}𝑃(Male and prefers pizza) & =\frac{35}{100} \\ & =0.35.\end{aligned}



$$

This represents the experimental probability that a randomly selected member prefers Italian pizza and is male.

### Example: Calculating the Compound Experimental Probability for an "AND" Event

#### Question

Anna surveys some students in a school, asking them what pets, out of cats or dogs, they have at home. Her findings are summarized in the two-way frequency table below.

Estimate the probability that a randomly selected student has a cat and a dog.

#### Explanation

To estimate the probability that a randomly selected student has a cat and a dog, we

- find the number in the cell where the row **** and the column **** meet, and then

- divide by the total number of data points in the table.

The cell where the row **** and the column **** meet is highlighted in the table below.

Dividing by the total number of data points in the table $(200),$ we get

$$



P(\text{Has a cat and dog}) = \dfrac{30}{200} = 0.15.



$$

### Compound "OR" Statements

Let's return to our first example, where the Volleyball club members were asked about whether they preferred Italian pizza or Chinese food. The data is shown below.

Suppose we want to find the probability that a randomly selected club member either prefers Chinese food *or* is female (or both).

By the formula for experimental probability,

$$



P(\text{Event}) = \dfrac{\text{number of event occurrences}}{\text{total number of trials}}



$$

to find this probability, we count the number of members who fall into at least one of these categories and divide by the total number of members in the table.

The cells that are in either the column **"Chinese food"** or the row **"Female"** (or both) are highlighted in the table below. Notice that where the row and column meet, we only count that group once.

Adding up these numbers and then dividing by the total number of data points in the table $(100),$ we get

$$



\begin{aligned}𝑃(Chinese food or Female) & =\frac{20+20+25}{100} \\ & =\frac{65}{100} \\ & =0.65.\end{aligned}



$$

This represents the experimental probability that a randomly selected member prefers Chinese food or is female.

### Example: Calculating the Compound Experimental Probability for an "OR" Event

#### Question

In a class, each student selected one of two possible courses, Math or Physics. The results are given in the two-way frequency table below.

Estimate the probability that a randomly chosen student selected Physics or is male, but not both.

#### Explanation

To estimate the probability that a randomly chosen student selected Physics or is male but not both, we

- add up the numbers that are in the column **** or the row ****, excluding the cell where these two categories overlap, and then

- divide by the total number of data points in the table.

The cells in the column **** or the row ****, excluding the cell where they overlap, are highlighted in the table below.

Adding up these numbers and then dividing by the total number of data points in the table $(50),$ we get

$$



P(\text{Physics or male, but not both}) =\dfrac{12+20}{50} = \dfrac{32}{50} =0.64.



$$

### Example: Making Predictions Using Compound Experimental Probabilities

#### Question

There are $250$ boys and $250$ girls in a school. Of them, $40$ boys and $40$ girls were randomly selected and asked which sport they prefer among volleyball, basketball, football, and soccer. The results of the survey are given in the two-way frequency table below.

Estimate the number of students in the entire school who are boys who prefer football or soccer.

#### Explanation

To estimate the probability that a randomly selected student is a boy who prefers football or soccer, we

- add up the numbers in the cells where the row **** and the columns **** and **** meet, and then

- divide by the total number of data points in the table.

The cells where the row **** and the columns **** and **** meet are highlighted in the table below.

Adding up these numbers and then dividing by the total number of data points in the table $(80),$ we get

$$



\begin{aligned}𝑃(Boy who prefers football or soccer) & =\frac{11+9}{80} \\ & =\frac{20}{80} \\ & =0.25.\end{aligned}



$$

This represents the estimated probability that a randomly selected student is a boy who prefers football or soccer.

Finally, to estimate the number of students in the entire school who are boys who prefer football or soccer, we multiply the total number of students in the school $(500)$ by the probability above:

$$



\begin{aligned}𝑛 & =500⋅𝑃(Boy who prefers football or soccer) \\ & =500⋅0.25 \\ & =125\end{aligned}



$$
