## Lesson

### Introduction

A rational number can have a decimal representation that either *ends* or *repeats*.

In this lesson, we will see how to convert rational numbers written as fractions into decimals using *long division*. We begin with examples where the decimal terminates.

Let's convert $\dfrac{7}{2}$ into a decimal using long division.

To write the fraction as a decimal, we divide the numerator by the denominator:

$$


7 \div 2


$$

We proceed using long division as usual, stopping once we obtain a remainder of $0\mathbin{:}$

Therefore,

$$


\dfrac{7}{2} = 3.5.


$$

### Example: Expressing a Rational Number as a Finite Decimal Using Long Division

#### Question

Convert $\dfrac{35}{4}$ into a decimal using long division.

#### Explanation

To write the fraction as a decimal, we divide the numerator by the denominator:

$$


35 \div 4


$$

We proceed using long division as usual, stopping once we obtain a remainder of $0$:

$$


\begin{aligned}8.7\,5\, \\ 4\,3\,5.0\,0 \\ \underset{}{−3\,2}.0\,0\,\, \\ 3.00\,\,\, \\ \underset{}{−2.8}\,0\,\, \\ 2\,0\,\, \\ \underset{}{−2\,0}\,\, \\ 0\,\,\end{aligned}


$$

Therefore,

$$


\dfrac{35}{4} = 8.75.


$$

### Repeating Decimals

Let's consider the following rational number:

$$


\dfrac13


$$

To find its decimal representation, we divide $1$ by $3$ using long division, as follows:

Notice that, in this case,

- the long division process never ends, and

- the digit $3$ after the decimal point repeats *forever*.

Therefore,

$$


\dfrac13 = 0.333\,333\ldots


$$

This is an example of a **repeating decimal** - a decimal in which the digits after the decimal point continue forever in a *repeating pattern*.

### Notations for Repeating Decimals

It's convenient to use the following special **bar-notation** to indicate decimals that repeat indefinitely:

$$


0.\overline{3} = 0.333\,333\ldots


$$

Some decimals have two or more digits that repeat indefinitely. For example:

$$


\begin{aligned}0.\overset{78}{} & =0.78\,78\,78\,78… \\ 0.\overset{675}{} & =0.675\,675\,675…\end{aligned}


$$

Some repeating decimals have non-repeating digits before the repeating part. For example:

$$


0.45\overline{67} = 0.45\,67\,67\,67\,67\ldots


$$

### Any Repeating Decimal is a Rational

The key takeaway for this lesson is that every repeating decimal is rational! In other words, every repeating decimal can be expressed as a ratio of two integers.

For example:

$$


\begin{aligned}0.\overset{3}{–} & =\frac{1}{3} \\ 0.\overset{78}{} & =\frac{26}{33} \\ 0.333\overset{67}{} & =\frac{16517}{49500}\end{aligned}


$$

In a future lesson, we'll learn how to express a repeating decimal as a fraction. For now, you can verify these results using a calculator.

### Example: Expressing a Rational Number as a Repeating Decimal Using Long Division

#### Question

Convert $\dfrac{79}{6}$ into a repeated decimal using long division.

#### Explanation

To write the fraction as a decimal, we divide the numerator by the denominator:

$$


79 \div 6


$$

We proceed using long division as usual, continuing until a repeating pattern of digits appears after the decimal point:

$$


\begin{aligned}1\,3.1\,6\,… \\ 6\,7\,9.0\,0… \\ \underset{}{−6}\,9.0\,0…\,\, \\ 1\,9.0\,0\,…\, \\ \underset{}{−1\,8}.0\,0…\,\, \\ 1.0\,0\,…\,\, \\ \underset{}{−0\,6}\,0\,…\,\, \\ 4\,\,0…\,\, \\ \underset{}{−3\,\,6}…\,\, \\ 4\,\,… \\ ⋮…\,\,\end{aligned}


$$

We could continue the division, but the same digit keeps repeating. Thus, the decimal continues with repeating $6$'s:

$$


13.16\ldots


$$

Therefore,

$$


\dfrac{79}{6} = 13.16\ldots = 13.1\overline{6}.


$$
