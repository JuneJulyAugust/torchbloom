### Introduction

Whenever we have an expression that's written as a difference of squares, we can factor the expression by applying the difference of squares formula:

$$


a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)


$$

Factoring a difference of squares works even when the numbers involved are not perfect squares! The key is to recognize that *any* positive number can be written as the square of its square root, even if that square root is irrational.

Let’s illustrate this idea by factoring the following expression:

$$


x^2 - 2


$$

To factor this expression, we first note that the $2$ can be expressed as a square:

$$


2 = \big(\sqrt{2}\big)^2


$$

This means we can write our expression $x^2-2$ as a difference of squares:

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑥^{2}−(\sqrt{√2})^{2} & \end{aligned}


$$

Finally, we factor the expression using the difference of squares formula with $a=x$ and $b=\sqrt{2}{:}$

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑥^{2}−(\sqrt{√2})^{2}=(𝑥−\sqrt{√2})(𝑥+\sqrt{√2})\end{aligned}


$$

Let's see some more examples.

### Example: Factoring a Difference of Squares

#### Question

Factor the expression $y^2-\dfrac{1}{10}.$

#### Explanation

Notice that

$$


10 = \big(\sqrt{10}\big)^2.


$$

First, we write the expression as a difference of squares:

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑦^{2}−\frac{1}{10} & = \\ 𝑦^{2}−\frac{1}{(\sqrt{√10})^{2}} & = \\ 𝑦^{2}−(\frac{1}{\sqrt{√10}})^{2} & \end{aligned}


$$

Then, we factor the expression using the difference of squares formula:

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑦^{2}−(\frac{1}{\sqrt{√10}})^{2}=(𝑦−\frac{1}{\sqrt{√10}})(𝑦+\frac{1}{\sqrt{√10}})\end{aligned}


$$

### Example: Factoring a Difference of Squares with Leading Coefficients

#### Question

Factor the expression $17t^2-13.$

#### Explanation

Notice that

$$


17 = \big(\sqrt{17}\big)^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 13 = \big(\sqrt{13}\big)^2.


$$

First, we write the expression as a difference of squares:

$$


\begin{aligned}17𝑡^{2}−13 & =(\sqrt{√17}\,𝑡)^{2}−(\sqrt{√13})^{2}\end{aligned}


$$

Then, we factor the expression using the difference of squares formula:

$$


\begin{aligned}(\sqrt{√17}\,𝑡)^{2}−(\sqrt{√13})^{2}=(\sqrt{√17}\,𝑡−\sqrt{√13})(\sqrt{√17}\,𝑡+\sqrt{√13})\end{aligned}


$$

### Example: Factoring a Difference of Squares Whose Terms Have a Common Factor

#### Question

Factor the expression $\dfrac{4m^2}{5}-36.$

#### Explanation

The terms of our expression have a common factor of $4.$ So, we simplify the expression by factoring it out:

$$


\dfrac{4m^2}{5}-36 = 4\left(\dfrac{m^2}{5}-9\right)


$$

Now, let's factor the expression inside parentheses using the difference of squares formula:

$$


\begin{aligned}\frac{𝑚^{2}}{5}−9 & = \\ (\frac{𝑚}{\sqrt{√5}})^{2}−3^{2} & = \\ (\frac{𝑚}{\sqrt{√5}}−3)(\frac{𝑚}{\sqrt{√5}}+3) & \end{aligned}


$$

Therefore, our original expression factors as follows:

$$


\dfrac{4m^2}{5}-36 = 4\left(\dfrac{m}{\sqrt5} - 3 \right)\left(\dfrac{m}{\sqrt5} + 3 \right)


$$

### Example: Factoring a Difference of Squares Containing More Than One Variable

#### Question

Factor the expression $-5r^2+ 36s^2.$

#### Explanation

Notice that

$$


5 = \big(\sqrt{5}\big)^2.


$$

First, we write the expression as a difference of squares:

$$


\begin{aligned}−5𝑟^{2}+36𝑠^{2} & = \\ 36𝑠^{2}−5𝑟^{2} & = \\ (6𝑠)^{2}−(\sqrt{√5}\,𝑟)^{2} & \end{aligned}


$$

Then, we factor the expression using the difference of squares formula:

$$


\begin{aligned}(6𝑠)^{2}−(\sqrt{√5}\,𝑟)^{2}=(6𝑠−\sqrt{√5}\,𝑟)(6𝑠+\sqrt{√5}\,𝑟)\end{aligned}


$$
