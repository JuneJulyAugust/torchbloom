### Introduction

The **power rule for exponents** states that a power raised to another power is equal to the product of the two powers:

$$


\left(a^m\right)^n = a^{mn}


$$

For example,

$$


\begin{aligned} \left(5^2\right)^3 &= \\5^{2 \cdot 3} &= \\5^6. \end{aligned}


$$

To see why the power rule for exponents is true, let's take the above example and work it out again, the long way:

$$


\begin{aligned} \left(5^2\right)^3 &= \\[5pt] \underbrace{5^2\cdot 5^2\cdot 5^2}_{3\text{ times}} &= \\[5pt] (5 \cdot 5) \cdot (5 \cdot 5) \cdot (5 \cdot 5) &= \\[5pt] \underbrace{5\cdot5\cdot5\cdot5\cdot5\cdot5}_{6\text{ times}} &= \\[5pt] 5^6 \end{aligned}


$$

The power rule for exponents is just a shortcut for the process above.

### Example: Using the Power Rule with Positive Exponents

#### Question

Find the value of $\left(x^4\right)^5.$

#### Explanation

We multiply the exponents, as follows:

$$


\begin{aligned} \left(x^4\right)^5 &= \\x^{4\cdot5} &= \\x^{20} \end{aligned}


$$

### Example: Using the Power Rule with Negative Exponents

#### Question

If $\left(x^{-3}\right)^{-6} = x^n,$ then what is $n?$

#### Explanation

We multiply the exponents, as follows:

$$


\begin{aligned} \left(x^{-3}\right)^{-6}&=\\x^{(-3)(-6)}&=\\x^{18} \end{aligned}


$$

Therefore $n=18.$

### The Power Rule With Fractional Exponents

Suppose we want to remove the radical term in an expression like $\sqrt{x^{3}}.$ To do this, we first convert the radical to a fractional exponent:

$$


\sqrt{x^{3}}= \left(x^{3}\right)^{1/2}


$$

Then, we can apply the power rule and multiply the fractional exponents:

$$


\begin{aligned}(𝑥^{3})^{1/2} & = \\ 𝑥^{(3)⋅(1/2)} & = \\ 𝑥^{3/2} & \end{aligned}


$$

Therefore,

$$


\sqrt{x^{3}} = x^{3/2},


$$

and in general,

$$


\sqrt[n]{x^m} = x^{m/n}.


$$

### Example: Using the Power Rule with Fractional Exponents

#### Question

Simplify $\sqrt[4]{x^{-8}}.$

#### Explanation

First, we convert the radical to a fractional exponent, and then we apply the power rule:

$$


\begin{aligned}\sqrt[√𝑥^{−8}]{4} & = \\ (𝑥^{−8})^{1/4} & = \\ 𝑥^{(−8)⋅(1/4)} & = \\ 𝑥^{−2} & = \\ \frac{1}{𝑥^{2}} & \end{aligned}


$$

### Example: Simplifying Challenging Expressions Using the Power Rule

#### Question

If $\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{x}}\right)^4 \cdot x = x^k$ for some constant $k$, then what is the value of $k?$

#### Explanation

First, we convert the radical to a fractional exponent, and then we apply the power rule:

$$


\begin{aligned}(\frac{1}{\sqrt[√𝑥]{3}})^{4}⋅𝑥 & = \\ (\frac{1}{𝑥^{1/3}})^{4}⋅𝑥 & = \\ (𝑥^{−1/3})^{4}⋅𝑥 & = \\ 𝑥^{(−1/3)⋅(4)}⋅𝑥 & = \\ 𝑥^{−4/3}⋅𝑥 & \end{aligned}


$$

Finally, we apply the product rule, adding the exponents:

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑥^{−4/3}⋅𝑥 & = \\ 𝑥^{−4/3}⋅𝑥^{1} & = \\ 𝑥^{−4/3+1} & = \\ 𝑥^{−4/3+3/3} & = \\ 𝑥^{−1/3} & \end{aligned}


$$

Therefore $k=-\dfrac{1}{3}.$
