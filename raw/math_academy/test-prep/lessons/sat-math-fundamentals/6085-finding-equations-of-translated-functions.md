## Lesson

### Introduction

In this lesson, we'll learn how to derive the equation of a translated function given the equation of some other function.

For example, consider a line defined by

$$


y = \underbrace{-x + 2}_{\large f(x)}.


$$

Let's find the equation of the line obtained by translating this line by $6$ units up.

For each point on the original line $y = f(x),$ we must add $6$ to each $y$-coordinate to get the corresponding point on the new line. So, the $y$-coordinates of the points on the new line are given by

$$


y_{\text{new}} = y + 6.


$$

We introduce the "$\text{new}$" suffix to distinguish $y$-values on the translated line from those on the original line.

Substituting the equation of our original line $y = -x + 2$ into the above equation gives

$$


y_{\text{new}} = (-x + 2) + 6.


$$

Since our equation no longer refers to points on the original line, we can drop the "$\text{new}$" suffix, which gives

$$


y = (-x + 2) + 6.


$$

Finally, collecting the like terms, we have

$$


y = -x + 8.


$$

Therefore, we conclude that if we translate the line $y = -x + 2$ by $6$ units *up*, we get the line $y = -x + 8.$

We can apply the same method to translate a quadratic function. Let's see how.

### Example: Vertical Translations

#### Question

A curve is defined by $y = f(x),$ where $f(x) = x^2 - 5x + 14.$ If $y = g(x)$ is a vertical translation of $y = f(x)$ by $6$ units down, what is the function $g(x)?$

#### Explanation

We're given the curve

$$


y = x^2 - 5x + 14


$$

and we wish to translate this curve by $6$ units down.

For each point on the curve $y = f(x),$ we must subtract $6$ from each $y$-coordinate to get the corresponding point on the new curve $y = g(x).$ So, the $y$-coordinates of the points on the new curve are given by

$$


y_{\text{new}} = y - 6.


$$

Substituting here the equation of the first curve gives

$$


y_{\text{new}} = (x^2 - 5x + 14) - 6.


$$

Dropping the $\text{new}$ suffix, we get

$$


y = (x^2 - 5x + 14) - 6.


$$

Finally, collecting the like terms, we have

$$


y = x^2 - 5x + 8.


$$

Therefore, $g(x) = x^2 - 5x + 8.$

### Horizontal Translations

Let's now see how to deal with horizontal translations.

For example, suppose a line is defined by

$$


y = \underbrace{-2x + 3}_{\large f(x)}.


$$

Let's find the equation of the line we get when we translate this line by $2$ units *left.*

For each point on the line $y = f(x),$ we must subtract $2$ from each $x$-coordinate to get the corresponding point on the new line. So, the $x$-coordinates of the points on the new line are given by

$$


x_{\text{new}} = x - 2.


$$

This equation is equivalent to

$$


x = x_{\text{new}} + 2.


$$

To get the equation of the new line, we substitute this into the equation of the first line. Doing this, we get

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑦 & =−2𝑥+3 \\ 𝑦 & =−2(𝑥_{new}+2)+3.\end{aligned}


$$

Dropping the $\text{new}$ suffix, we get

$$


y = -2(x + 2) + 3.


$$

Finally, simplifying this expression using the distributive law and collecting like terms, we get

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑦 & =−2𝑥−4+3 \\ 𝑦 & =−2𝑥−1.\end{aligned}


$$

### Example: Horizontal Translations of Linear Functions

#### Question

A line is defined by $y = f(x),$ where $f(x) = 4x + 2.$ If $y = g(x)$ is a horizontal translation of $y = f(x)$ by $1$ unit right, what is the function $g(x)?$

#### Explanation

We're given the line

$$


y = 4x + 2


$$

and we wish to translate this line by $1$ unit right.

For each point on the line $y = f(x),$ we must add $1$ to each $x$-coordinate to get the corresponding point on the new line $y = g(x).$ So, the $x$-coordinates of the points on the new line are given by

$$


x_{\text{new}} = x + 1.


$$

This equation is equivalent to

$$


x = x_{\text{new}} - 1.


$$

To get the equation of the new line, we substitute this into the equation of the first line. Doing this, we get

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑦 & =4𝑥+2 \\ 𝑦 & =4(𝑥_{new}−1)+2.\end{aligned}


$$

Dropping the $\text{new}$ suffix, we get

$$


y = 4(x - 1) + 2.


$$

Finally, simplifying this expression gives

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑦 & =4𝑥−4+2 \\ 𝑦 & =4𝑥−2.\end{aligned}


$$

Therefore, $g(x) = 4x - 2.$

### Example: Horizontal Translations of Quadratic Functions

#### Question

A curve is defined by $y = f(x),$ where $f(x) = -x^2 + x + 2.$ If $y = g(x)$ is a horizontal translation of $y = f(x)$ by $2$ units left, what is the function $g(x)?$

#### Explanation

We're given the curve

$$


y = -x^2 + x + 2


$$

and we wish to translate this curve by $2$ units left.

For each point on the curve $y = f(x),$ we must subtract $2$ from each $x$-coordinate to get the corresponding point on the new curve $y = g(x).$ So, the $x$-coordinates of the points on the new curve are given by

$$


x_{\text{new}} = x - 2.


$$

This equation is equivalent to

$$


x = x_{\text{new}} + 2.


$$

To get the equation of the new curve, we substitute this into the equation of the first curve. Doing this, we get

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑦 & =−𝑥^{2}+𝑥+2 \\ 𝑦 & =−(𝑥_{new}+2)^{2}+(𝑥_{new}+2)+2.\end{aligned}


$$

Dropping the $\text{new}$ suffix, we get

$$


y = -(x + 2)^2 + (x + 2) + 2.


$$

Finally, simplifying the expression, we have

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑦 & =−(𝑥+2)^{2}+(𝑥+2)+2 \\ 𝑦 & =−𝑥^{2}−4𝑥−4+𝑥+2+2 \\ 𝑦 & =−𝑥^{2}−3𝑥.\end{aligned}


$$

Therefore, $g(x) = -x^2 - 3x.$

### Lines in Standard Form

Now, suppose we have the following line, given in standard form:

$$


x + y = -4


$$

Suppose we translate this line by $2$ units up. We can find the equation of the new line using the same technique as before!

For each point on the line, we must add $2$ to each $y$-coordinate to get the corresponding point on the new line. So, the $y$-coordinates of the points on the new line are given by

$$


y_{\text{new}} = y + 2.


$$

This equation is equivalent to

$$


y = y_{\text{new}} - 2.


$$

To get the equation of the new line, we substitute this into the equation of the first line. Doing this, we get

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑥+𝑦 & =−4 \\ 𝑥+(𝑦_{new}−2) & =−4.\end{aligned}


$$

Dropping the $\text{new}$ suffix, we get

$$


x + (y - 2) = -4.


$$

Finally, simplifying and writing this equation in standard form, we get

$$


\begin{aligned}𝑥+(𝑦−2) & =−4 \\ 𝑥+𝑦−2 & =−4 \\ 𝑥+𝑦 & =−2.\end{aligned}


$$

Let's now consider a situation where we must perform a horizontal translation of a line given in standard form.

### Example: Translating Lines Given in Standard Form

#### Question

The line $3x + 2y = 1$ is translated by $4$ units left. What is the equation of the new line?

#### Explanation

We're given the line

$$


3x + 2y = 1


$$

and we wish to translate this line by $4$ units left.

For each point on the line, we must subtract $4$ from each $x$-coordinate to get the corresponding point on the new line. So, the $x$-coordinates of the points on the new line are given by

$$


x_{\text{new}} = x - 4.


$$

This equation is equivalent to

$$


x = x_{\text{new}} + 4.


$$

To get the equation of the new line, we substitute this into the equation of the first line. Doing this, we get

$$


\begin{aligned}3𝑥+2𝑦 & =1 \\ 3(𝑥_{new}+4)+2𝑦 & =1.\end{aligned}


$$

Dropping the $\text{new}$ suffix, we get

$$


3(x + 4) + 2y = 1.


$$

Finally, simplifying and writing this equation in standard form, we get

$$


\begin{aligned}3𝑥+12+2𝑦 & =1 \\ 3𝑥+2𝑦 & =−11.\end{aligned}


$$
