## Lesson

### Introduction

In this lesson, we'll discuss strategies for combining exponential expressions.

For example, suppose we wish to simplify the following expression:

$$


\dfrac{7}{5}(9^r) + 0.9(9^r)


$$

First, notice that the terms of the expression contain a common factor of $9^r,$ so let's factor this out:

$$


\left(\dfrac{7}{5}+0.9\right) \cdot 9^r


$$

Finally, we simplify the terms in the parentheses by converting the decimal to a fraction and then adding the fractions.

$$


\begin{aligned}(\frac{7}{5}+0.9)⋅9^{𝑟} & =(\frac{7}{5}+\frac{9}{10})⋅9^{𝑟} \\ & =(\frac{14}{10}+\frac{9}{10})⋅9^{𝑟} \\ & =\frac{23}{10}(9^{𝑟}).\end{aligned}


$$

Therefore, we conclude that

$$


\dfrac{7}{5}(9^r) + 0.9(9^r) = \dfrac{23}{10}(9^r).


$$

### Example: Factoring Exponential Expressions

#### Question

Simplify the expression $\dfrac{7}{8}\left(2^{-s}\right) - \left(2^{-s}\right).$

#### Explanation

We need to write the expression

$$


\dfrac{7}{8}\left(2^{-s}\right) - \left(2^{-s}\right)


$$

in the form $k\left(2^{-s}\right).$

Factoring out the common factor of $\left(2^{-s}\right)$ in the expression, we have

$$


\begin{aligned}\frac{7}{8}(2^{−𝑠})−(2^{−𝑠}) & =\frac{7}{8}⋅(2^{−𝑠})−1⋅(2^{−𝑠}) \\ & =(\frac{7}{8}−1)⋅(2^{−𝑠}) \\ & =(\frac{7}{8}−\frac{8}{8})⋅(2^{−𝑠}) \\ & =(−\frac{1}{8})⋅(2^{−𝑠}) \\ & =−\frac{1}{8}(2^{−𝑠}).\end{aligned}


$$

### The Rules of Exponents

In the work that follows, it will be useful to remember the rules of exponents:

- Negative exponents: $x^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{x^n}$

- Radicals as fractional exponents: $\sqrt[n]{x} = x^{1/n}$

- The product rule: $a^n \cdot a^m = a^{n+m}$

- The quotient rule: $\dfrac{a^n}{a^m} = a^{n-m}$

- The power rule: $\left(a^m\right)^n = a^{mn}$

- The power of product rule: $(ab)^n = a^n b^n$

- The power of quotient rule: $\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^n = \dfrac{a^n}{b^n}$

Let's see some more examples.

### Example: Using the Product and Quotient Rules to Simplify Expressions

#### Question

Express $\dfrac{64}{27}\left(\dfrac43\right)^t$ as a power of $\dfrac43$ only.

#### Explanation

Let's recall the rules of exponents:

- Negative exponents: $x^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{x^n}$

- Radicals as fractional exponents: $\sqrt[n]{x} = x^{1/n}$

- The product rule: $a^n \cdot a^m = a^{n+m}$

- The quotient rule: $\dfrac{a^n}{a^m} = a^{n-m}$

- The power rule: $\left(a^m \right)^n = a^{mn}$

- The power of product rule: $(ab)^n = a^nb^n$

- The power of quotient rule: $\left(\dfrac a b \right)^n = \dfrac{a^n}{b^n}$

Now, our expression

$$


\dfrac{64}{27}\left(\dfrac43\right)^t


$$

has an additional factor of $\dfrac{64}{27}.$ However, notice that

$$


64=4^3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 27=3^3


$$

are both perfect cubes. Therefore, we can write our expression as

$$


\dfrac{4^3}{3^3}\left(\dfrac43\right)^t.


$$

By the power of the quotient rule, we have

$$


\left(\dfrac43\right)^3\left(\dfrac43\right)^t.


$$

Finally, we apply the product rule:

$$


\begin{aligned}(\frac{4}{3})^{3}(\frac{4}{3})^{𝑡} & =(\frac{4}{3})^{𝑡+3}\end{aligned}


$$

### Example: Using Power Rule to Simplify Expressions

#### Question

Express $5^{2k}\,4^{k}$ in the form $n^k,$ where $n$ is an integer.

#### Explanation

Let's recall the rules of exponents:

- Negative exponents: $x^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{x^n}$

- Radicals as fractional exponents: $\sqrt[n]{x} = x^{1/n}$

- The product rule: $a^n \cdot a^m = a^{n+m}$

- The quotient rule: $\dfrac{a^n}{a^m} = a^{n-m}$

- The power rule: $\left(a^m \right)^n = a^{mn}$

- The power of product rule: $(ab)^n = a^nb^n$

- The power of quotient rule: $\left(\dfrac a b \right)^n = \dfrac{a^n}{b^n}$

Now, our expression

$$


5^{2k}\,4^{k},


$$

involves the factor $5^{2k}.$ However, using the power rule, we can express this as a power of $k$ as follows:

$$


5^{2k} = \left(5^{2}\right)^k


$$

Thus, we can rewrite the given expression as follows:

$$


\begin{aligned}5^{2𝑘}\,4^{𝑘} & =(5^{2})^{𝑘}\,4^{𝑘}\end{aligned}


$$

Since $5^2 = 25,$ we have

$$


\left(5^{2}\right)^k \, 4^k = 25^k \, 4^k.


$$

Finally, using the power of the product rule, we can write this as

$$


25^k \, 4^k = \left(100\right)^k.


$$

Hence, $n = 100.$

### Example: Adding and Subtracting Exponential Expressions

#### Question

Given that $15(3^{x-1}) - 4(3^x) = k(3^x),$ find the value of $k.$

#### Explanation

Let's recall the rules of exponents:

- Negative exponents: $x^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{x^n}$

- Radicals as fractional exponents: $\sqrt[n]{x} = x^{1/n}$

- The product rule: $a^n \cdot a^m = a^{n+m}$

- The quotient rule: $\dfrac{a^n}{a^m} = a^{n-m}$

- The power rule: $\left(a^m \right)^n = a^{mn}$

- The power of product rule: $(ab)^n = a^nb^n$

- The power of quotient rule: $\left(\dfrac a b \right)^n = \dfrac{a^n}{b^n}$

We need to write the expression

$$


15(3^{x-1}) - 4(3^x)


$$

in the form $k(3^x).$

First, we rewrite the first term using the product rule and negative exponents:

$$


\begin{aligned}15(3^{𝑥−1})−4(3^{𝑥}) & =15⋅(3^{𝑥}⋅3^{−1})−4(3^{𝑥}) \\ & =15⋅(3^{𝑥}⋅\frac{1}{3})−4(3^{𝑥}) \\ & =15⋅(\frac{1}{3}⋅3^{𝑥})−4(3^{𝑥}) \\ & =5(3^{𝑥})−4(3^{𝑥})\end{aligned}


$$

Finally, combining the two negative terms, we have

$$


\begin{aligned}5(3^{𝑥})−4(3^{𝑥}) & =(5−4)3^{𝑥} \\ & =1⋅3^{𝑥} \\ & =3^{𝑘}.\end{aligned}


$$

Therefore, $k=1.$
