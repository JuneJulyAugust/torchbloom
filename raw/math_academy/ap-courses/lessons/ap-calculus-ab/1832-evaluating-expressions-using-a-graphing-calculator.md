### Evaluating an Expression Using a Calculator

Suppose we want to evaluate the following expression using a scientific calculator:

$$


\dfrac{\pi}{e-1}


$$

Using parentheses when inputting the denominator $e-1$ into a calculator is *essential*. This is because $\pi \div e - 1$ is equivalent to

$$


\dfrac{\pi}e - 1


$$

which is *not* the same as the expression we want to evaluate!

So, let's begin by rewriting our expression using parentheses:

$$


\dfrac{\pi}{e-1} = \pi \div \boxed{(}e - 1\boxed{)}


$$

We're now ready to enter this expression into a calculator. To do so, we note the following:

- We enter the symbols from left to right as they appear.

- Your calculator will have special $\boxed{\color{gray}\,(\,}$ and $\boxed{\color{gray}\,)\,}$ buttons for left and right parentheses.

- Scientific and graphing calculators usually have special $\boxed{\color{gray}\,e\,}$ and $\boxed{\color{gray}\,\pi\,}$ buttons. You may need to press $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{2nd}}$ or $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{SHIFT}}$ to access these.

Evaluating this using a calculator, we obtain

$$


\pi \div (e - 1) \approx 1.828


$$

rounded to $3$ decimal places.

When evaluating expressions on a scientific or graphing calculator, we always round to $3$ decimal places unless stated otherwise.

### Example: Using Parentheses to Evaluate Rational Expressions

#### Question

Evaluate $\dfrac{e+7}{\pi-1}.$

#### Explanation

Recall that we always round to $3$ decimal places unless stated otherwise.

Our expression can be written as follows:

$$


\dfrac{e+7}{\pi-1} = {\boxed{(}e+7\boxed{)}} \div {\boxed{(}\pi-1\boxed{)}}


$$

We're now ready to enter this expression into a calculator. To do so, we note the following:

- We enter the symbols from left to right as they appear.

- Your calculator will have special $\boxed{\color{gray}\,(\,}$ and $\boxed{\color{gray}\,)\,}$ buttons for left and right parentheses.

- Scientific and graphing calculators usually have special $\boxed{\color{gray}\,e\,}$ and $\boxed{\color{gray}\,\pi\,}$ buttons. You may need to press $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{2nd}}$ or $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{SHIFT}}$ to access these.

Notice the parentheses in the expression above.

Evaluating this using a calculator, we obtain

$$


(e+7) \div (\pi-1) \approx 4.538


$$

rounded to $3$ decimal places.

### The Negative Sign Button

Scientific and graphing calculators usually have a special "negative sign" button for negative numbers. It's usually a minus sign enclosed in parentheses $\boxed{\color{gray}(-)}.$

This is *not* the same as the "subtract" button, which is usually written as a minus sign $\boxed{\color{gray}\,-\,}$ without parentheses. Confusing the two can lead to errors.

Let's evaluate the following expression using a calculator, rounding our answer to five decimal places:

$$


5^{-1-e}


$$

To evaluate this expression using a calculator, we note the following:

- To raise a number to a power, you need to use the $\boxed{\color{gray}\,\wedge\,}$ button. On some calculators, this might be an $\boxed{\color{gray}\,x^y\,}$ button, or similar. You may need to press $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{2nd}}$ or $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{SHIFT}}$ to access this.

- The first number in the exponent is $-1,$ so we use the "negative sign" button $\boxed{\color{gray}(-)}$ when entering this number.

- We then *subtract* $e$ from $-1,$ so we use the "subtract" button $\boxed{\color{gray}\,-\,}$ for this operation.

Therefore, we'd use a sequence of buttons similar to the following to evaluate this expression:

$$


\boxed{\color{gray}\,5\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,\land\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}(-)} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,1\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,-\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,e\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,=\,}


$$

Evaluating our expression using a scientific or graphing calculator, we obtain

$$


5^{-1-e} \approx 0.002\,52


$$

rounded to $5$ decimal places.

**Watch out!** In the sequence of buttons above, we assume that pressing the exponent button moves the cursor upwards into a superscript. If your calculator does not do this, then it is likely necessary to enclose the exponent in parentheses:

$$


\boxed{\color{gray}\,5\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,\land\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,(\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}(-)} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,1\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,-\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,e\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,)\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,=\,}


$$

### Example: Using the Negative and Subtract Buttons

#### Question

Evaluate $2^{-1-2\pi}.$

#### Explanation

Recall that we always round to $3$ decimal places unless stated otherwise.

Note the following:

- Calculators usually have a special "negative sign" button $\boxed{\color{gray}(-)}$ for negative numbers. This is not the "subtract" button.

- Scientific and graphing calculators usually have a $\boxed{\color{gray}\,\pi\,}$ button for $\pi.$ You may also need to press $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{2nd}}$ or $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{SHIFT}}$ to access it.

- To raise a number to a power, you need to use the $\boxed{\color{gray}\,\wedge\,}$ button or the $\boxed{\color{gray}\,x^y\,}$ button (or similar). Again, you may need to press $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{2nd}}$ or $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{SHIFT}}$ to access it, and you may need to enclose the exponent in parentheses.

Evaluating this using a calculator, we obtain

$$


2^{-1-2\pi} \approx 0.006


$$

rounded to $3$ decimal places.

### Evaluating Radicals

Most calculators have a special $\boxed{\color{gray}\,\sqrt{\phantom{0}}\,}$ button for the square root. However, many calculators do not have a special symbol for other radicals.

If your calculator *does* have a button for other radicals, it will look similar to $\boxed{\color{gray}\,\sqrt[n]{x}\,}.$

An alternative way to compute the numerical value of a radical is to rewrite the expression as a fractional exponent and then use the exponent button. Common symbols used for the exponent button are $\boxed{\color{gray}\,\land\,},$ $\boxed{\color{gray}\,x^y\,},$ or similar.

Let's use the exponent button to evaluate the following expression to $5$ decimal places:

$$


\sqrt[7]{38}


$$

Firstly, our radical can be written as a fractional exponent as follows:

$$


\sqrt[7]{38} = 38^{\boxed{1/7}}


$$

So, we'd use a sequence of buttons similar to the following to evaluate this expression:

$$


\boxed{\color{gray}\,3\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,8\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,\land\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,1\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,\div\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,7\,}


$$

Therefore, we obtain

$$


38^{1/7} \approx 1.681\,45


$$

rounded to $5$ decimal places.

**Watch out!** In the sequence of buttons above, we assume that pressing the exponent button moves the cursor upwards into a superscript. If your calculator does not do this, then it is likely necessary to enclose the exponent in parentheses:

$$


\boxed{\color{gray}\,3\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,8\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,\land\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,(\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,1\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,\div\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,7\,} \qquad \boxed{\color{gray}\,)\,} \qquad


$$

### Example: Using Exponents to Compute Radicals

#### Question

Evaluate $\sqrt[5]{1331}.$

#### Explanation

Recall that we always round to $3$ decimal places unless stated otherwise.

Note the following:

- Most calculators have a special $\boxed{\color{gray}\sqrt{\phantom{a}}}$ button for the square root.

- Not all calculators have a special button for other radicals. If your calculator does have a radicals button, it will look similar to $\boxed{\color{gray}\,\sqrt[n]{x}\,}\,.$

- An alternative way to compute the numerical value of a radical is to rewrite the expression as a fractional exponent and then use the exponent button.

- Remember that to raise a number to an exponent, you need to use the $\boxed{\color{gray}\,\wedge\,}$ button or the $\boxed{\color{gray}\,x^y\,}$ button (or similar). You may also need to press $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{2nd}}$ or $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{SHIFT}}$ to access it, and you may need to enclose the exponent in parentheses.

Our expression can be written using exponents as follows:

$$


\sqrt[5]{1331} = 1331^{\boxed{1/5}}


$$

Evaluating this using a calculator, we obtain

$$


1331^{1/5} \approx 4.215


$$

rounded to $3$ decimal places.

### Putting a Calculator Into Degrees Mode

When evaluating trigonometric expressions using a calculator, it is *vital* to ensure that the calculator is in the correct mode before starting.

For example, let's consider the following expression:

$$


\sin(\pi^\circ)


$$

There is a "degree sign" $({}^\circ)$ next to the argument $\pi.$ This means we must ensure that our calculator is in **degrees mode**.

A calculator is in degrees mode if $\boxed{\textrm{DEG}},$ $\boxed{\textrm{DEGREE}},$ or $\boxed{\textrm{D}}$ is shown somewhere on the calculator's display. If you cannot see this, you must first put your calculator into degrees mode.

We can usually put a calculator in degrees mode in one of the following ways:

- On scientific calculators, you can usually access degrees mode by pressing $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{DRG}}$ or $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{MODE}}$ and selecting $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{DEG}}$ from the menu. You may also need to press $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{2nd}}$ or $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{SHIFT}}$ to access the menu.

- On graphing calculators, you can usually access degrees mode by pressing $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{MODE}}$ and selecting $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{DEGREE}}$ from the menu.

If neither of these techniques works, your calculator's manual will show you how. Or, you can search online for a video specific to your calculator model.

Once the calculator is in degrees mode, we can safely evaluate our expression using the calculator:

$$


\sin(\pi^\circ) \approx 0.055


$$

rounded to three decimal places.

### Putting a Calculator Into Radians Mode

Let's now evaluate the following expression:

$$


\sin(13)


$$

In this case, there is no "degree sign" $({}^\circ)$ next to the argument $13.$ This means we must evaluate this trigonometric expression with our calculator in **radians mode**.

A calculator is in radians mode if $\boxed{\textrm{RAD}},$ $\boxed{\textrm{RADIAN}},$ or $\boxed{\textrm{R}}$ is shown somewhere on the calculator's display. If you cannot see this, you must first put your calculator into radians mode.

We can usually put a calculator in radians mode in one of the following ways:

- On scientific calculators, you can usually access radians mode by pressing $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{DRG}}$ or $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{MODE}}$ and selecting $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{RAD}}$ from the menu. You may also need to press $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{2nd}}$ or $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{SHIFT}}$ to access the menu.

- On graphing calculators, you can usually access radians mode by pressing $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{MODE}}$ and selecting $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{RADIAN}}$ from the menu.

If neither of these techniques works, consult your calculator's manual or check for video explanations.

Once the calculator is in radians mode, we can safely evaluate our expression:

$$


\sin(13) \approx 0.420


$$

rounded to $3$ decimal places.

### Example: Switching Between Degrees and Radians Mode

#### Question

Evaluate $\tan(135)$ using a calculator.

#### Explanation

Recall that we always round to $3$ decimal places unless stated otherwise.

Note the following:

- Since there is no "degree sign" $({}^\circ)$ next to the $135,$ we're required to evaluate this trigonometric expression using radians. So, make sure that your calculator is in $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{RAD}}$ (radians) mode.

- On scientific calculators, you can usually access radians mode by pressing $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{DRG}}$ or $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{MODE}}$ and selecting $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{RAD}}$ from the menu. You may also need to press $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{2nd}}$ or $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{SHIFT}}$ to access the menu.

- On graphing calculators, you can usually access radians mode by pressing $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{MODE}}$ and selecting $\boxed{\color{gray}\textrm{RADIAN}}$ from the menu.

Evaluating this using a calculator, we obtain

$$


\tan(135) \approx -0.089


$$

rounded to $3$ decimal places.
