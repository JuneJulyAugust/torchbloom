### Introduction

To divide square roots, we simply divide the numbers under the root. This is called the **quotient rule for radicals**.

To demonstrate, let's simplify the expression $\dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{\sqrt{2}}$ by applying the quotient rule:

$$


\dfrac{ \sqrt{10} }{ \sqrt{2} } = \sqrt{ \dfrac{10}{2} } = \sqrt{5}


$$

The quotient rule also works for cube roots. For example,

$$


\dfrac{ \sqrt[3]{10} }{ \sqrt[3]{2} } = \sqrt[3]{ \dfrac{10}{2} } = \sqrt[3]{5}


$$

In general, the quotient rule works for any quotient of $n$th roots. So for any $n,$ we have

$$


\dfrac{ \sqrt[n]{10} }{ \sqrt[n]{2} } = \sqrt[n]{ \dfrac{10}{2} } = \sqrt[n]{5}.


$$

**Watch out!** We cannot use the quotient rule when we have the quotient of different radicals, such as square roots and cube roots. For example, the expression $\dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{\sqrt[3]{5}}$ cannot be simplified because the radicals $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ and $\sqrt[3]{\phantom{x}}$ are different.

### Example: Separating a Square Root Into a Quotient of Two Square Roots

#### Question

Express $\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{7}}$ as the quotient of two radicals.

#### Explanation

Applying the quotient rule, we have

$$


\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{7}}=\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{7}} .


$$

### Example: Writing a Quotient of Two Square Roots as a Single Square Root

#### Question

Express $\dfrac{\sqrt{12}}{\sqrt{3}}$ as a single radical.

#### Explanation

Using the quotient rule, we have

$$


\dfrac{\sqrt{12}}{\sqrt{3}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{12}{3}}= \sqrt{4} = 2 .


$$

### Example: The Quotient Rule With Cube Roots

#### Question

Express $\dfrac{\sqrt[3]{3}}{\sqrt[3]{4}}$ as a single radical.

#### Explanation

Applying the quotient rule, we have

$$


\dfrac{\sqrt[3]{3}}{\sqrt[3]{4}} = \sqrt[3] {\dfrac{3}{4}} .


$$
